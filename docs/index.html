What this leaderboard measures

Stolen bases are usually credited to speed. Most of the time that's right — but not always. Josh Naylor stole 30 bases at 94% while running slower than 98% of the league. This model strips sprint speed out of base-stealing and shows what's left: the part a runner actually created, and the part a coach can actually move. Every number below is measured against runners of the same sprint speed, so 0 always means "exactly what your wheels predict."

Steal+ Headline · production
Net bases (SB − CS) a runner adds above what an average runner of his exact sprint speed would produce over the same number of attempts. Volume-aware — 30 bags from a slow body counts. Because your speed already buys ~80% success, a caught stealing costs about 4× what a steal earns.
Steal+ = (SB − CS) − attempts × (2p − 1) = 2(1−p)·SB − 2p·CS
0 = exactly what his speed predicts · + = skill adds bases · p = speed-expected success rate ()
Burst Coachable · process
Feet of ground the runner gains off the base between the pitcher's first move and the pitcher's release (his secondary lead), above what his speed predicts. Measured before any steal outcome exists — it's the jump and lead itself. Nearly uncorrelated with Steal+, so it's a genuinely separate read.

A stolen base is a race between two clocks.

The runner's clock starts the instant the pitcher commits, and ends when he touches second. The catcher's clock starts when the ball hits his mitt. Everything this site measures lives inside those two clocks — and almost none of it is sprint speed.

The anatomy of one steal attempt
The three numbers, in the order they happen
Ground gainedStep 1 · what he did
How many feet did he win before the pitch left the hand?

The raw measurement — the blue stretch above. From the pitcher's first move until the pitcher's release. On any single pitch this is the strongest lever there is: each extra foot multiplies his odds of being safe by about 1.36×.

Ground gained = secondary lead − primary lead  (feet)
But it is not a fair ranking. Slower runners naturally gain more ground — they take bigger secondary leads to compensate. Raw ground gained correlates -0.48 with sprint speed, so it quietly flatters slow runners. Hence step 2.
BurstStep 2 · vs his own speed
Was that more ground than his legs should have won?

Exactly the same measurement, with the speed effect removed: subtract the ground a runner of his sprint speed typically gains. What's left is the part he created — the jump and the lead, judged fairly across fast and slow runners.

Burst = ground gained − ground his speed predicts
Speed-neutral by construction (correlation with sprint speed: 0.00). Measured before any outcome exists, so it cannot be luck.
Steal+Step 3 · the result
Did all of that turn into bases?

His net bases (steals minus caught stealings) minus what an average runner of his exact sprint speed would have produced on the same number of tries. The outcome, with speed removed the same way.

Steal+ = (SB − CS) − what his speed alone predicts
A caught stealing costs about 4× what a steal earns, so this rewards clean volume rather than reckless attempts.

…and what he's running against
—
Pop time
Catch to second base — the catcher's transfer plus throw. League average shown; roughly 1.85 s is elite, 2.05 s is slow. Lower is tougher to run on.
—
Arm strength
Max-effort throw velocity to second. Higher is tougher. Pop time and arm are why two identical jumps can end differently.
—
League ground gained
The average feet won between the pitcher's first move and his release. Beat this for your speed and your Burst is positive.
Every runner row on the leaderboard carries the average pop time and arm he actually faced, so a big Steal+ against tough catchers is not the same as a big Steal+ against weak ones.
